1. Research objective and classification tasks

I developed the technical and experimental system for the I2C-UHU submission to EXIST 2024, addressing sexism identification and source-intent classification in Spanish and English tweets. These are related but distinct problems: a message can contain sexist content because it expresses discrimination, reports an experience or criticizes that behavior. Detecting a reference to sexism is therefore insufficient to infer the position taken by its author. I structured the experiments around this distinction rather than treating every positive example as an endorsement of sexist language.

Task 1 predicts YES or NO. Task 2 distinguishes DIRECT, REPORTED and JUDGEMENTAL within the sexist-content branch, retaining NO at the hierarchical output level. The objective was to compare architectures, select models using development evidence and produce the hard decisions and soft outputs required by the shared task. The research contribution combines multilingual classification, analysis of subjective annotations and a reproducible sequence of baseline, optimization and evaluation experiments.

Paper, §§1, 3.1–3.2

2. Dataset structure and annotation analysis

I worked with the organizers’ JSON files: 6,920 training tweets, comprising 3,660 Spanish and 3,260 English instances, and 1,038 development tweets, comprising 549 Spanish and 489 English instances. Each record associates the text and language with six annotations and demographic descriptors. I examined class frequencies, language distribution and the gender and age groups represented in the annotations, because these affect both model specialization and the interpretation of disagreement.

The framework distinguishes the supplied development partition from the internal training/validation subsets used for different model versions. I prepared language-specific and annotation-group-specific datasets instead of assuming that one aggregation would suit every task. A hard reference collapses annotations to a selected category; a soft reference preserves their distribution. Keeping both representations made it possible to investigate consensus-based classification alongside perspectivist models. Demographic groupings describe annotation perspectives, not additional independent tweets or a count of unique annotators.

Paper, §§3.3, 4.2

3. Baselines and architecture comparison

I first compared four pretrained Transformer families: XLM-RoBERTa Base, DeBERTa v3 Base, Spanish RoBERTa Base BNE and multilingual BERT. Baseline A learned NO, DIRECT, REPORTED and JUDGEMENTAL jointly. Baseline B separated binary sexism detection from the subsequent intent classifier. This comparison tested whether the hierarchy should be learned in a single output space or expressed explicitly as two decisions. It also exposed the difficulty of minority intent categories, which a favorable score for NO could conceal.

The initial configuration used batches of 32, learning rate 2e-5, maximum sequence length 128 and weight decay 0.01. For the binary baseline, XLM-RoBERTa reached F1 0.7807; its three-class baseline reported F1 0.568331. These are development experiments, not official test results. Scores for four-class, binary and three-class formulations answer different questions and should not be interpreted as a single directly comparable improvement percentage. The comparison informed the subsequent two-stage design and the selection of candidate models.

Paper, §4.1, Tables 3–5

4. Text cleaning and normalization

I implemented the preprocessing described in the paper: lowercase conversion and removal of links, user mentions, retweet markers, hashtags and emojis. The intention was to reduce account-specific and formatting variation before classification. Emoji verbalization was also explored; the article reports that it did not improve the experiments, so emojis were removed in the selected preparation. These decisions belong to this experimental configuration and are not a general claim that such information is irrelevant to sexism or intent.

The preprocessing stage had to be interpreted alongside error analysis. Removing social-media markers can simplify inputs while losing contextual clues, particularly in quoted statements or ironic criticism. I therefore describe normalization as a modeling choice with a trade-off, rather than as a guarantee of better semantics. Language-aware model selection remained important after cleaning: Spanish-specific pretraining and English-specific pretraining provide different representations even when the surface preparation follows the same rules.

Paper, §§4.3, 4.6

5. Class balancing and hyperparameter search

To address intent-class imbalance, I expanded REPORTED and JUDGEMENTAL through backtranslation while keeping DIRECT at its original size. I used Helsinki-NLP/OPUS translation models to generate lexical and syntactic variants. Spanish examples followed Spanish → English → German → Spanish; English examples followed English → German → Spanish → English. This increased the variety available to minority classes without simply repeating identical rows. It also introduced a limitation: translation can change pragmatic meaning, especially in sarcasm and culturally specific expressions.

I then used Optuna to explore batch sizes 8, 16 and 32; learning rates 3e-5 and 5e-5; and weight decay 0.001, 0.01 and 0.1. The search used reduced training and validation subsets to control computational cost. In the paper’s reported comparison, XLM-RoBERTa’s binary F1 moved from 0.7807 to 0.7876 after augmentation and optimization. This is evidence for that combined experiment, not an isolated estimate of augmentation’s causal effect. The configuration table below separates the initial baseline from the later search space.

6. Training and language-specific models

I implemented training with Hugging Face Trainer and the adamw_torch optimizer, evaluating at epoch boundaries, retaining checkpoints and selecting the model according to F1. The initial experiments allowed up to ten epochs, with an early-stopping patience of three. The published work reports execution on an RTX 4070. This setup supported repeated comparisons without assuming that a fixed final epoch would always give the best classifier. Model selection was based on validation evidence rather than the official leaderboard.

For Task 1, I compared a single multilingual XLM-RoBERTa with language-specific classifiers: DeBERTa v3 for English and RoBERTa BNE for Spanish. The latter combination generated run I2C-UHU_2. The paper reports an internal combined F1 of 0.8617 for this version; it must be kept separate from the official positive-class F1 of 0.7733. Different partitions and evaluation settings prevent interpreting those numbers as interchangeable measurements of deployment accuracy.

Paper, §4.5, Tables 12–15

7. Ensembles and learning with disagreement

For intent classification I developed versions that retained multiple annotation perspectives. Version v2.2 trains six XLM-RoBERTa instances using groups differentiated by gender and age: three female and three male age groups. Their predictions are combined through an ensemble. Version v2.3 compares this with an ensemble built only from the three female groups. The purpose is to study how the representation of different perspectives changes predictions, rather than to treat every disagreement as an annotation mistake.

The annotation distribution provides a soft reference: two votes for a category among six annotations correspond to 2/6 support. The hierarchy also connects the stages: an intent probability is conditioned by the probability of sexist content, expressed as P(intent) = P(YES) × P(intent | YES). Hard decisions select categories; soft outputs represent graded support across them. The six-group version generated the strongest official intent run, I2C-UHU_2. This comparison supports analysis of the tested ensembles, without establishing a universal causal claim about demographic diversity.

Paper, §4.5.2, Tables 16–17

8. Official evaluation, errors and limitations

I evaluated the systems through the official Hard-Hard and Soft-Soft settings. ICM determines leaderboard order; F1 and cross entropy provide complementary views. For run I2C-UHU_2, Task 1 ranked 10th in Hard-Hard and 13th in Soft-Soft; Task 2 ranked 11th and 17th respectively. These are run positions in the corresponding ALL leaderboards, not ranks among unique teams. The results table preserves the task, evaluation setting, run identifier and metric so that each figure can be checked against its source.

I examined misclassifications and confusion matrices to identify confusion between direct sexism, reporting and criticism. Sarcasm, idioms, cultural context and ambiguous wording remained difficult. The work demonstrates implementation of multilingual training, data augmentation, hyperparameter optimization and ensemble evaluation, while its evidence remains limited to the benchmark and published experiments. Additional data and alternative ensemble strategies were proposed as future research; they are not presented here as completed implementations or as a production moderation system.

1. Research objective and three evaluation tasks

I developed the technical and experimental pipelines for the I2C-UHU-Altair submission to EXIST 2025, extending the text-based work to Spanish and English memes. Image and text can change each other’s meaning: a visually neutral scene may become discriminatory through its caption, while an apparently sexist statement may be used to criticize discrimination. The system therefore needed to model their interaction, not merely classify isolated words or recognize objects. The research compared multimodal feature extraction with a description-based language-model route.

Subtask 2.1 identifies sexist content. Subtask 2.2 distinguishes DIRECT from JUDGEMENTAL intent; unlike the tweet task, it excludes REPORTED. Subtask 2.3 predicts five non-exclusive sexism categories: ideological inequality, stereotyping and dominance, objectification, sexual violence, and misogyny/non-sexual violence. A meme can belong to multiple categories. This distinction is essential when designing outputs and interpreting probabilities: multiclass intent and multilabel categorization do not share the same normalization constraint.

Paper, §§1, 3.1–3.3

2. Dataset and experimental partitions

The supplied dataset contains 4,044 training memes and 1,053 test memes in JSON format, linking each image to extracted text, language, identifiers and multiple annotations. I organized the inputs so that the textual and visual representations referred to the same example throughout processing. The internal experimental split used 3,235 training and 809 validation instances. This validation subset is distinct from the official test set and is the appropriate context for model selection and hyperparameter comparison.

The reported split stratified examples by their dominant label. For multilabel data, this provides one practical stratification criterion, but it does not guarantee preservation of every label co-occurrence. I also analyzed annotation distributions and the prevalence of overlapping categories. The article reports extensive disagreement in binary annotations and frequent assignment of multiple sexism types. These observations motivated probabilistic outputs and complementary experiments with consensus-selected examples, while requiring caution about what a single hard label can represent.

Paper, §§3.4–3.5, 3.7

3. Text and image preparation

I implemented the published textual preparation: lowercasing, removal of irrelevant special characters, whitespace normalization and exclusion of empty text instances. The pipeline retained informative punctuation when appropriate. For images, I verified file existence and validity, converted inputs to RGB, standardized image formatting and prepared the visual values for model processing. These operations address practical data integrity issues before embedding extraction or generative inference; a missing image cannot be silently treated as a complete multimodal example.

I treated preprocessing as part of the experimental architecture. The feature-based pipeline needs consistent vector inputs, whereas the description-based route must preserve the visual and textual cues that explain the meme’s social meaning. Cleaning reduces formatting variation, but aggressive removal can discard useful context. The paper’s preprocessing description should therefore be read as the implemented preparation, rather than evidence that each individual transformation improves every task. Subsequent error analysis remains necessary to identify information lost or misunderstood downstream.

Paper, §3.6

4. Disagreement, consensus and example curation

I implemented different annotation-processing strategies for the two pipelines. In Pipeline A, consensus labels were computed for the binary and intent tasks while excluding invalid or UNKNOWN responses. The paper describes a frequency threshold of at least 50% for selecting category labels in its curation procedure. This is an internal modeling choice, not a statement of the organizer’s official gold-label thresholds. I also compared annotations against the aggregated reference using accuracy and, for multilabel outputs, Jaccard similarity.

For Pipeline B, I selected highly agreed examples as few-shot references: the curation function identifies up to ten representative candidates per class, while prompts use a smaller set of three to five examples. Agreement makes those examples easier for a language model to interpret, but it is not equivalent to objective correctness. Ranking annotators against consensus can undervalue valid minority perspectives. The technical account therefore distinguishes disagreement modeling, consensus aggregation and prompt-example selection instead of presenting them as an identical operation.

Paper, §4.3, Table 1

5. Pipeline A: BLIP-2 representations and XGBoost

I implemented the BLIP-2 feature-extraction route, combining visual representations with textual representations. The published architecture describes the vision encoder and Q-Former as the bridge for visual information, followed by sequence preparation, L2 normalization and attention pooling to obtain fixed-size representations. The pooled image and text vectors are concatenated before supervised prediction. This separates multimodal representation extraction from the downstream estimator and makes it possible to compare classifier configurations without redefining the input representation at every step.

The reported XGBoost configuration uses squared-error regression, 300 trees, maximum depth 10, learning rate 0.05 and L2 regularization 1, with GPU execution. Grid search also explores subsample and colsample_bytree. The paper contains differing descriptions of output normalization; the report does not turn those into an unsupported implementation claim. In particular, the official multilabel task permits category probabilities whose sum exceeds one. A sum-to-one distribution must not be presented as a universal requirement for Subtask 2.3. Pipeline A corresponds to run I2C-UHU-Altair_1.

Paper, §§4.1.1, 4.4.2

6. Pipeline B: Qwen descriptions and few-shot classification

I implemented a second route using Qwen/Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct to generate concise semantic descriptions from each meme. The prompt focuses on the situation and its social or gender implications instead of requesting a general visual inventory. The resulting description is passed to instruction-tuned language models for classification, using selected examples to demonstrate the task. The article discusses Mistral-family models and other named variants; the report does not assume that every variant shares the same base architecture.

Few-shot prompting places examples in the inference context; it is not task-specific fine-tuning of model weights. That distinction separates this route from the trained text baselines and the supervised XGBoost estimator. The approach exposes an interpretable intermediate description, but its errors can propagate: omitted visual context or a mistaken interpretation constrains the classifier’s subsequent decision. This route generated I2C-UHU-Altair_2, evaluated as hard predictions. Its performance should not be described as soft calibration where no corresponding official soft submission exists.

Paper, §4.2

7. Baselines and evaluation protocol

I used text-only BERT-base and XLM-RoBERTa-base baselines to establish a reference against which to examine multimodal approaches. Their published training setup uses AdamW, learning rate 3e-5, batch size 32, sequence length 128, weight decay 0.01 and five epochs, with early stopping based on validation behavior. This configuration belongs to those Transformer baselines, not automatically to Qwen inference or XGBoost. Keeping those roles explicit makes the comparison technically interpretable.

Hard-Hard compares selected categories with an aggregated reference. Soft-Soft compares predictions against the distribution of human annotations. ICM is the official ranking metric; normalized ICM is a relative evaluation score, not a probability or percentage accuracy. F1 YES complements binary results, while macro-F1 summarizes class-level performance for intent and categorization. The table below uses ALL instances from the organizer’s final leaderboards. It identifies each run and evaluation mode, avoiding a mixture of internal validation scores, language-specific subsets and official test results.

8. Official results, interpretation and limitations

Pipeline A ranked 5th in detection, 2nd in intent and 3rd in categorization under Soft-Soft evaluation. The intent result follows the official leaderboard, which resolves the inconsistent 3rd-place statement in the article’s abstract. The strongest Hard-Hard positions were 9th for detection with Pipeline B, 7th for intent with Pipeline A and 6th for categorization with Pipeline A. Their F1 values were respectively 0.7125 for YES, 0.2685 macro-F1 and 0.4223 macro-F1. Positions refer to submitted runs in each track.

The findings support comparison of two different ways to use visual information, not a claim that one method dominates all settings. Error analysis highlights irony, implicit discrimination, minority categories and description fidelity as remaining limitations. The work demonstrates multimodal input preparation, representation fusion, supervised estimation, prompt design and benchmark evaluation. Video, audio, real-time moderation and additional model families are future directions described by the paper; they are not completed capabilities of these meme-classification systems.
